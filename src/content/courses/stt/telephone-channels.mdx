The source recordings are already 8 kHz. They cannot demonstrate which high-frequency information was lost during an earlier conversion from wideband audio. Upsampling them produces more samples, not new acoustic evidence.

## Controlled Changes

Use our narrowband examples to study added distortion, noise and level changes. Use a separate wideband recording if your question is the effect of downsampling. Keep each transform separate before combining them.

| Experiment | Keep fixed | Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Added noise | Original clip and decoder | Which digits change at the chosen SNR? |
| Level change | Noise and transcript | Does preprocessing handle quiet audio? |
| Codec round trip | Sample rate and framing | What does this codec implementation alter? |
| Downsampling | A genuine wideband source | What changes after anti-alias filtering? |

An 8 kHz WAV is not automatically a telephone call. A real path can add endpoint processing, packet loss, transcoding and competing speech. The [phone-channel walkthrough](/blog/building-a-phone-line-in-pytorch) develops a controlled approximation and its limits.

## Noise Probe

For a reproducible full-clip SNR probe:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(17)
noise = rng.standard_normal(audio.shape).astype('float32')
noise /= np.sqrt(np.mean(noise**2))
signal_rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(audio**2))
noisy = audio + noise * signal_rms * 10**(-10/20)
```

This requests 10 dB using RMS across the entire clip, including its silence. It does not describe speech-active SNR. Do not silently clip or renormalise the mixture: those operations change the experiment.

## Compare Outputs

Listen to the clean and transformed clip at a sensible playback volume. Decode both with the same checkpoint and retain both hypotheses. A worsening prediction is one observation, not a robustness curve.
