## 8. CTC, attention, and transducers: the three decoder families

Every recognition model must solve one alignment problem: audio frames arrive at 100 per second, words arrive at 2 or 3 per second, and nothing says which frames belong to which word. The three decoder families are three answers, and each answer has production consequences.

| Family                    | How it aligns                                                                                                       | Production character                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| CTC                       | Predicts a label or blank per frame, then collapses repeats and blanks into text. Each frame decided independently. | Fast, simple, naturally streaming. Weak language modelling of its own, so it leans on external biasing. Spelling of rare words suffers.                                                                                  |
| Attention encoder-decoder | Encodes all audio, then generates text token by token attending over the whole encoding.                            | Strongest accuracy and context use. Wants the full utterance before finalising, so streaming is grafted on rather than native. Can hallucinate fluent text on silence or noise, because it is a text generator at heart. |
| Transducer                | Joint network over an audio encoder and a text-history predictor emits tokens as audio arrives.                     | The streaming production default: native low latency with internal language modelling. More complex to train and serve than CTC.                                                                                         |

<Pull>
  The hallucination modes differ by family, and knowing which family you are running tells you what
  to guard against: CTC fails toward garbled spellings, attention models fail toward confident
  fluent inventions, transducers sit between.
</Pull>

<Practice>
  When a provider is cagey about architecture, their behaviour on two probes tells you most of it:
  transcribe pure silence and transcribe heavy noise. A model that returns fluent sentences for
  either is attention-family behaviour, and it needs a no-speech guard in front of it before an
  agent trusts its output.
</Practice>

## 9. Streaming versus batch, and why they trade accuracy

The single largest fork in recognition is whether the model transcribes as audio arrives or after it ends, and the fork is architectural, not a toggle.

**Batch** models see the entire utterance, including everything after each word, before committing to any of it. Future context resolves ambiguity constantly in speech, which is why batch accuracy is the ceiling: the model that waits knows more.

**Streaming** models commit with bounded lookahead, typically a few hundred milliseconds of future audio at most. Less context at each decision means more errors, structurally, not incidentally. The compensations, chunked attention, limited-future encoders, transducer decoding, narrow the gap without closing it.

Streaming output arrives as **partials**, provisional transcripts that revise as more audio lands, followed by **finals**. Two consequences for agent design: downstream stages can start on partials but must tolerate revision, and the delay before a segment is marked final, finalisation latency, sits directly inside the turn-taking budget from the components pages.

<Practice>
  A pattern worth stealing: run streaming recognition for the live turn, then re-transcribe the same
  audio in batch mode afterwards for the record. The agent gets speed, the transcript archive and
  analytics get accuracy, and the delta between the two becomes a free ongoing measurement of your
  streaming penalty.
</Practice>

## 10. Language models, biasing, and custom vocabulary

Acoustics alone cannot transcribe speech, because speech is ambiguous. Recognise speech and wreck a nice beach are nearly identical acoustically; only knowledge of language separates them. Every recognition system carries that knowledge somewhere, and where it lives determines what you can change without retraining.

**Implicit language modelling** lives inside end-to-end models: attention decoders and transducer predictors learn word statistics from training transcripts. Powerful, and fixed at training time.

**Biasing** is the runtime lever: supplying words and phrases the model should favour right now. Implementations vary, boosted vocabularies, contextual phrase lists, shallow fusion with an external language model, but the shape is constant: you are tilting the decoder's preferences without touching its weights.

Biasing is also **dynamic**, and that is its production superpower: the phrase list can change per call, per turn, per screen. An agent asking for a medication name can bias toward the formulary for exactly that turn. A support line can load the caller's product list at connect time.

<Pull>
  Biasing is the highest-leverage accuracy tool that does not involve training, and the least used
  relative to its value. The twenty domain terms your business depends on are precisely the words a
  general model has barely seen, and precisely the words biasing fixes this afternoon.
</Pull>

Its limits define the boundary with unit 5: biasing tilts choices among plausible transcriptions. It cannot conjure acoustic evidence the model never learned to hear, which is where dialect and accent adaptation, genuine fine tuning, take over.

<Practice>
  Before any fine-tuning conversation, exhaust biasing: build the domain phrase list, wire it per
  context, and measure entity accuracy before and after, unit 4's harness again. Half of vocabulary
  problems end here, at a fraction of the cost, and the half that remain are now cleanly diagnosed
  as acoustic rather than lexical.
</Practice>
