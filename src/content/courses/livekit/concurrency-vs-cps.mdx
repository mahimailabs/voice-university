Every load test on the project held a target concurrency: 150, 300, 500, 600. Months of runs, tens of thousands of calls, a capacity model fitted to per-node measurements.

Then someone asked what our calls-per-second (CPS) ceiling was. We did not know, and none of the runs could tell us.

## Traffic Relationship

For a stable workload, average active calls equal the answered-call rate times average connected duration. This is a steady-state relationship, not an instantaneous burst formula:

```text
average active calls = answered calls per second x average connected duration
```

This is [Little's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little%27s_law) applied to calls, and it hides a gap. Our harness derived the arrival rate from the concurrency and hold time we asked for. Rate was never a dial.

That is good design for repeatable capacity runs, and it meant we had covered a range of rates only by accident: 0.33 CPS on the soak up to 2.0 CPS on the burst. What we had never done is hold concurrency flat and push the rate up, which helps isolate setup pressure.

## Capacity Axes

Calls per second stresses setup: signalling, transaction state, room creation, agent dispatch, worker pool spawn. Concurrency stresses carrying: media relay, ports, bandwidth, steady CPU.

The same concurrency figure can hide a tenfold difference in setup load:

| Concurrency | Average call length | Required CPS |
| ----------- | ------------------- | ------------ |
| 500         | 5 minutes           | 1.67         |
| 500         | 30 seconds          | 16.7         |

Identical media load. Ten times the setup work. A fleet that holds 500 five-minute calls comfortably has told you nothing about 500 thirty-second calls.

## Setup Bottlenecks

The agent tier failed at 50 concurrent calls, and the failure was not media. The generator timed out waiting for the answer, because the worker pool was spawning processes and could not do it fast enough.

It was an arrival-rate failure wearing a concurrency costume. We found it only because a ramp happened to push the rate up alongside the concurrency. Read as a concurrency result, "fails at 50" would have sent us looking at ports and memory, where the [per-call slopes](/learn/100x/livekit/livekit-agent-memory-pss-rss) said there was plenty of room.

Worker pool spawn is a setup cost. The Python agents framework keeps some processes warm ahead of demand, controlled by `num_idle_processes` (see the [server options](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/server/options/) docs), which is one of the first knobs to look at when setup is what times out.

## CPS Experiment

Hold concurrency flat and raise the arrival rate in steps. To keep concurrency constant, shorten the hold time as the rate goes up, so rate times duration stays the same.

Here is an example step plan for 500 concurrent. The first and last rows are the two cases above; the middle rows are filled in with the same arithmetic:

| Step | Target CPS | Hold time per call | Concurrency |
| ---- | ---------- | ------------------ | ----------- |
| 1    | 1.67       | 300 s (5 minutes)  | 500         |
| 2    | 5          | 100 s              | 500         |
| 3    | 10         | 50 s               | 500         |
| 4    | 16.7       | 30 s               | 500         |

Hold each step long enough to reach steady state before moving on, and watch the setup side while you do: time from INVITE to answer, answer timeouts, dispatch latency and worker spawn. The step where setup starts to fail is your CPS ceiling at 500 concurrent. Keep the audio duty cycle and model behaviour comparable. Shorter calls can change greeting frequency and speech activity, so inspect those before attributing a change solely to setup.

The rig itself (real calls, per-call RTP counts, a fleet-side collector) is the one from [how to load test a voice agent](/learn/100x/livekit/voice-agent-load-testing). Both axes then feed the [capacity model](/learn/100x/livekit/voice-capacity-model), which keeps setup and carrying as separate columns.

<Practice label="When you report capacity">
  State both numbers whenever you state capacity, and say which one you varied. 500 concurrent is
  one point on a two-dimensional surface, and on its own it says nothing about arrival rate. If you
  have only measured one axis, say so. The number is still useful; it is just not the number
  somebody should plan an arrival-rate ceiling from.
</Practice>
