A run at 75 concurrent failed 83 of 400 calls. We bucketed the failures by minute and read the shape: low at the start, rising through the middle, falling at the end. A hump. A hump in the middle of a flat-rate run means the system got overwhelmed and recovered. That is a capacity story, and we wrote it up as one.

It was wrong, and the error was in the timestamp we grouped by.

The numbers below are saved case-study results. The original raw run bundle is not included here, and this review did not rerun the fleet experiment.

## Event Timestamps

A call that fails has a start time and an end time, and they can be minutes apart. We bucketed by the minute each call ended. Calls lasted about two minutes, so every failure was recorded two minutes after the condition that caused it.

That shift is enough to move a peak. Failures caused by conditions early in the run land in the middle of the chart. The first minutes look quiet because few calls have ended yet. By the last minutes, the calls ending were started after the trouble had passed. What you get is a hump built out of a lag.

## Start Cohorts

Grouped by the minute each call started, the same 83 failures look like this:

| Minute | Calls started | Failed |
| ------ | ------------- | ------ |
| 09:07  | 9             | 1      |
| 09:08  | 30            | 14     |
| 09:09  | 30            | 17     |
| 09:10  | 30            | 17     |
| 09:11  | 30            | 15     |
| 09:12  | 30            | 12     |
| 09:13  | 30            | 5      |
| 09:14  | 30            | 0      |
| 09:15  | 32            | 0      |

Most complete minutes in this excerpt offered 30 calls; boundary buckets differ. Read down the right-hand column: failures rise initially, then decline to zero. That supports a warm-up hypothesis; it does not prove the cause without a controlled rerun.

Compare offered rate, achieved concurrency, call duration and readiness between runs. Those controls matter more than the shape of one curve; lower load in a passing rerun would not, by itself, rule out capacity.

## Warm-Up Hypothesis

The difference matters because each reading prescribes a different action.

- **"Ran out of capacity"** means buy more machines.
- **"Warming up"** means pre-warm the pool before traffic arrives, which uses idle compute and memory.

Pre-warming was the actual fix. A rerun at identical settings completed 400 of 400.

<Pull>
  The end-time chart would have sent someone shopping for hardware they did not need. The start-time
  chart pointed at pool readiness rather than immediate fleet expansion.
</Pull>

If the pool in question is a LiveKit agent worker pool, the framework already runs each job in its own process and can keep processes warm ahead of demand: a `prewarm` function loads slow assets before any job is assigned, and `num_idle_processes` controls how many warm processes wait. Both are documented under [server options](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/server/options/). The rate side of the same problem, where the pool cannot spawn fast enough, is the subject of [concurrency vs calls per second](/learn/100x/livekit/concurrency-vs-cps).

## Attribution Windows

Bucket by the timestamp of the event you are attributing, not the timestamp you happen to have. Use start cohorts for setup failures. For mid-call loss or stalls, use the actual failure timestamp and contemporaneous load. The end time is useful for completion and teardown questions.

Most load tools and call records hand you the end time first, because that is when the record is written. Check which one your report uses before you read any shape into it.

If your call records sit in a pandas DataFrame with a `started_at` timestamp and a boolean `failed` column, the fix is one line: floor the start timestamp to the minute (pandas [`Series.dt.floor`](https://pandas.pydata.org/docs/reference/api/pandas.Series.dt.floor.html)) and group on that.

```python
by_start = calls.groupby(calls["started_at"].dt.floor("min"))["failed"].agg(["count", "sum"])
```

The gap between the two clocks is the call duration, and at a fixed arrival rate longer calls also mean more of them in flight at once ([Little's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little%27s_law) again, the same relation behind [concurrency versus calls per second](/learn/100x/livekit/concurrency-vs-cps)). The longer your calls, the further an end-time chart drifts from the moment that caused it.

<Practice label="Check your own reports">

1. Find the timestamp your failure chart groups by. If it is an end, hangup, or completion time, re-bucket by start time.
2. Plot offered load (calls started per minute) on the same axis. A shape in failures means little until you know the load was flat.
3. If failures decay under flat load, rerun with the pool pre-warmed before concluding anything about capacity.

</Practice>
