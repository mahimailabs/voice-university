LJSpeech gives us one speaker, paired transcripts and about 24 hours of audio. Before choosing training clips, inspect the recordings: sample rates, levels, silence and transcript consistency.

## Corpus Contents

LJSpeech is a public-domain English speech corpus of 13,100 short clips read by one speaker, Linda Johnson, with a transcript for each. Its [dataset page](https://keithito.com/LJ-Speech-Dataset/) describes the audio as recorded for the LibriVox project in 2016 and 2017, from seven non-fiction books published between 1884 and 1964.

Three details on that page matter for training:

- The clips "were segmented automatically based on silences in the recording", so clip boundaries usually, but not always, fall on sentence or clause boundaries.
- The source recordings were MP3 files, so the page warns they "may contain artifacts introduced by the MP3 encoding."
- Each transcript comes twice: raw, and normalised with numbers, ordinals, money and abbreviations spelled out.

It is the standard single-speaker English benchmark. The pretrained vocoder we use and the Piper voice we compare against later were both trained on it, which is why we build on it too.

## Audio Measurements

A clean TTS corpus is consistent: one sample rate, one channel, steady loudness, short and even edge silence, and no clipping. Here is LJSpeech 1.1 against each of those, measured by `measure_corpus.py` over all 13,100 clips on a 4-core cloud container.

| Measurement                | LJSpeech 1.1                                                  |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Clips / total audio        | 13,100 / 23.9 h                                               |
| Sample rate, channels      | 22,050 Hz only, mono only                                     |
| Clip duration              | min 1.11 s, p5 2.68 s, median 6.76 s, p95 9.72 s, max 10.10 s |
| Integrated loudness (LUFS) | p5 -25.2, median -23.3, p95 -21.1 (spread 4.1 dB)             |
| Median peak level          | -4.9 dBFS                                                     |
| Leading silence            | median 0.00 s, max 0.12 s                                     |
| Trailing silence           | median 54 ms, p95 77 ms                                       |
| Clips with clipped samples | 2 (one sample each)                                           |
| Transcript size            | median 17 words, 102 characters; 222,524 words total          |

Each row answers a question the model will otherwise answer for you. The measurement function per clip is short (key lines, lightly condensed):

```python
# measure_corpus.py
TOP_DB = 40  # anything 40 dB below the clip's peak counts as silence
CLIP = 0.999  # a sample at or above this absolute value is treated as clipped

meter = pyln.Meter(sr)
lufs = float(meter.integrated_loudness(audio)) if dur >= 0.4 else float("nan")
_, (start, end) = librosa.effects.trim(audio, top_db=TOP_DB)
clipped = int(np.sum(np.abs(audio) >= CLIP))
```

Loudness comes from [pyloudnorm](https://github.com/csteinmetz1/pyloudnorm), an implementation of ITU-R BS.1770-4. Silence comes from `librosa.effects.trim` with a 40 dB threshold; librosa's default is 60 dB, so we are stricter about what counts as sound.

## Loudness Consistency

Loudness consistency matters because a TTS model learns loudness from the data like everything else. If some clips are recorded 10 dB hotter than others, the model learns that the voice's level drifts, and it will drift at inference with nothing in the text to explain why.

LJSpeech's loudness spreads 4.1 dB from the 5th to the 95th percentile. Integrated loudness in LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale) weights frequencies roughly the way hearing does, which is why we measure it instead of peak level. Peak tells you headroom: a median of -4.9 dBFS leaves room, and only two samples in the whole corpus reach 0.999.

## Edge Silence

Edge silence matters because the model learns to reproduce it. Long, uneven leading or trailing silence teaches a model to pause unpredictably at the start and end of its output, and it inflates the time before the first audible sound.

LJSpeech arrives already trimmed. The median clip starts speaking immediately, the longest leading silence is 0.12 s, and trailing silence has a median of 54 ms and a 95th percentile of 77 ms. That short, even tail suggests the dataset author trimmed deliberately and left a consistent release. Pauses inside the sentence are untouched, and they should be: they carry phrasing.

## Dataset Limits

LJSpeech does not give you variety, and for a single voice that is mostly the point. Recognition models want many speakers and conditions; a synthesis model wants one voice held steady for hours. Still, three limits are worth knowing before you build on it:

- **Style.** It is audiobook narration. A model trained on it narrates. Short conversational turns for a voice agent are a different speaking style.
- **Vocabulary.** The source texts are old non-fiction, including the report on the Kennedy assassination. Modern product names, URLs and phone numbers are rare or absent.
- **Written numbers.** 1,505 clips (11.5%) have a raw transcript that differs from its normalised one, and 1,489 contain digits. LJ002-0013 reads "1808" as "eighteen oh eight". [Text normalisation](/learn/100x/tts/text-normalisation-tts) covers why that matters, and [forced alignment](/learn/100x/tts/forced-alignment-tts) points at those clips as where most low-confidence alignments cluster.

We did not train on all 23.9 hours. For the CPU budget of this build we aligned the first 571 clips, kept 458 after filtering, and trained on 413 of them: 0.74 h. [The data manifest](/learn/100x/tts/tts-data-manifest) shows how those clips are recorded for training.
