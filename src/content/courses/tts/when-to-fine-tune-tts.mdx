Hand our tiny model the sentence "call me at 5 pm" and the 5 is gone before the first layer runs. We trained it for 2,200 steps on a 4-core cloud container with no GPU, and no number of extra steps would bring the digit back. The fix belongs in the text front end.

## Frontend Failures

Because many errors happen before the model sees anything. If the text front end deletes, mangles or misreads a token, the acoustic model is faithfully speaking the wrong input.

Here is the tokenizer we actually ran, from `common.py`:

```python
SYMBOLS = ["<pad>", " ", "'"] + [chr(c) for c in range(ord("a"), ord("z") + 1)]
SYM2ID = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(SYMBOLS)}

def encode(text: str) -> list[int]:
    return [SYM2ID[c] for c in text if c in SYM2ID]
```

Anything outside those 29 symbols is silently dropped. Digits, currency signs and punctuation all vanish. We made that choice on purpose to keep the model small, and it shows how large the front end's share of the work is. In the raw LJSpeech metadata, 1,505 of 13,100 clips (11.5%) have a transcript that differs from the normalised one, and 1,489 of those contain digits. The dataset ships a normalised column alongside the raw one; its [dataset page](https://keithito.com/LJ-Speech-Dataset/) describes it as numbers, ordinals and monetary units expanded into words.

More steps did not improve held-out mel L1 either. Validation mel L1 went from 0.4268 at step 1,650 to 0.4282 at step 2,200, and mean output WER on our three probes moved from 0.21 to 0.35 over the same stretch. This suggests a plateau under that training setup; it does not isolate data size, optimisation or model capacity as the cause. [Training from scratch](/learn/100x/tts/train-tts-model-from-scratch) has the full curve.

## Cheaper Fixes

Try the cheap levers in order of cost, and re-run your evaluation after each one:

1. **Text normalisation.** Numbers, dates, units, codes and abbreviations expanded into speakable words. Most number and date misreadings end here. See [text normalisation](/learn/100x/tts/text-normalisation-tts).
2. **Pronunciation dictionaries.** A product name or drug name gets a phonetic entry. This only works if the model takes phoneme input or exposes an override; NeMo's [G2P documentation](https://docs.nvidia.com/nemo-framework/user-guide/latest/nemotoolkit/tts/g2p.html) notes that phonetic input lets you "enforce the desired pronunciation" at inference time.
3. **Model controls.** Speaking rate, emphasis and style tags, where the model has them.
4. **A different stock voice or model.** Auditioning a handful of existing voices through your real audio path, including the telephone path, is cheaper than any training run.

Each of these is measured in minutes or hours of engineering. A fine-tune is measured in data collection, compute and a new evaluation cycle.

## Manual Corrections

Take your list of failures, fix ten of them by hand with a normaliser rule or a dictionary entry, and run the list again. The result sorts the problem into one of two kinds.

**The list gets shorter and stays short.** You have a dictionary problem. The failures were individual items, and each fix removed one. Keep fixing by hand; it scales linearly with the number of words you care about, and that number is usually finite.

**New failures keep arriving at the same rate.** You have a distribution problem. The model has not learned a pattern, so every new sentence exposes another instance of it. No per-word fix catches up with an unbounded list.

Our digit-dropping model sits on the first branch. A normaliser that turns "5" into "five" roughly halved its hard-set WER (64.4% to 35.6% at step 1,650), because the letters were always in the model's vocabulary. It left phone numbers, leading zeros and "pm" wrong: those need a better normaliser, not a fine-tune.

## Training Criteria

Fine-tuning earns its cost when the failure is systematic and the fix has to live in the weights. The common cases:

- **A language, dialect or accent** that the base model renders as its majority neighbour. A dictionary entry cannot change a vowel system or a rhythm.
- **One specific voice** that has to stay stable across long sessions, beyond what prompt-based cloning holds. [Voice cloning vs fine-tuning](/learn/100x/tts/voice-cloning-vs-fine-tuning) compares the two.
- **A domain register** such as clinical dictation or legal citation, where vocabulary and rhythm are unusual together.
- **A data boundary.** Self-hosting may be required when audio cannot leave your infrastructure. First test an existing local model; deployment restrictions alone do not establish a need to fine-tune.

Even then, write the acceptance bar before you train: a target output WER on a test set built from the failures, a listening sign-off, and a latency ceiling. The [eval harness](/learn/100x/tts/tts-eval-harness) is where those numbers come from.
