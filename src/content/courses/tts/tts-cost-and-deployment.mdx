At a real-time factor of 0.052, Piper produced one second of audio in about 52 ms on our 4-core cloud container with no GPU. Keep that machine busy for an hour and it would produce about 1,150 minutes of audio. That single division is the start of every self-hosted TTS cost model, and the rest of the model is about why you will not get all 1,150.

By the end of this page you will be able to turn a measured real-time factor into a cost per minute with a placeholder price, see how utilisation moves it, and run the deployment and licence checks that decide whether you can ship at all. We quote no vendor or hardware prices; the formula is the deliverable.

## How do you calculate TTS cost per minute?

Divide the machine's hourly price by the minutes of audio it produces per hour at your real operating point. With real-time factor RTF, one fully busy hour produces 60 / RTF minutes of audio, so:

```text
cost per audio minute = P × RTF / (60 × U)

P   = hourly price of the machine (your placeholder)
RTF = seconds of compute per second of audio, measured on that machine
U   = utilisation, the fraction of paid time spent synthesising (0 to 1)
```

Here are the real-time factors we measured, all on the same 4-core container, one request at a time, with the audio minutes per busy hour derived from them:

| System (measured on 4 CPU cores, concurrency one) | RTF          | Audio minutes per busy hour (60 / RTF) | Cost per minute at U = 1 |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Piper `en_US-ljspeech-medium`                     | 0.052        | about 1,150                            | P × 0.00087              |
| Our tiny model (step 1,650) + HiFi-GAN            | 0.24         | 250                                    | P × 0.004                |
| HiFi-GAN vocoder alone, across runs               | 0.19 to 0.27 | about 220 to 320                       | P × 0.0032 to P × 0.0045 |

The Piper row comes from three probe sentences. On the 61 sentences of the [eval harness](/learn/100x/tts/tts-eval-harness), the same voice on the same container measured RTF 0.037. We keep 0.052 as the worked example, but the choice of test changes the number, so measure on text like yours.

The third column is arithmetic on the second, not a throughput test. Two things we did not measure would change it: running several requests at once, which can raise total throughput or collapse per-request speed depending on the machine, and GPU hardware, where the numbers are different in kind. Measure RTF at your target concurrency before you trust the column.

Where does the money go? The table hints at it. In our cascade the vocoder took 96.4% of synthesis time, so any cost work on that pipeline starts with the vocoder. The [neural vocoder lesson](/learn/100x/tts/what-is-a-neural-vocoder) has the breakdown.

## Why is utilisation the biggest number in the formula?

Because you pay for the machine whether it is speaking or waiting. Cost per minute scales with 1 / U: at U = 0.25, Piper's P × 0.00087 becomes P × 0.0035, four times higher, with no change to the model.

Voice traffic makes high utilisation hard. Calls cluster by time of day, and an agent that must answer instantly cannot queue requests to fill the gaps. Three things push U down in practice:

- **Warm capacity for latency.** A service that scales to zero pays a cold start on the first request. Keeping a floor of warm instances protects [time to first audio](/learn/100x/tts/ttfa-vs-ttfb) and costs money every idle hour.
- **Headroom for peaks and failures.** Capacity sized for the busiest minute sits partly idle the rest of the day, and redundancy doubles some of it.
- **Per-session slots.** If your serving stack reserves a worker per call, that worker sits idle between replies and while audio plays.

The comparison with a hosted API follows from the same terms. If you keep N instances warm all month, your floor is N × P × (hours in the month). A hosted service charging A per minute costs A × V for V minutes. The break-even volume is V = N × P × hours / A, before engineering time. Below it, hosted wins on cost; above it, self-hosting can, if the utilisation you assumed is the utilisation you get.

## What changes for in-region and on-prem TTS?

The audio stops being allowed to leave. Often that constraint, rather than cost, is what moves a team to self-host.

**In-region** deployment runs the same serving stack inside a named jurisdiction. The model is the easy part. Telephony numbers, media paths, logs and recordings all need to stay in the region too, and the audit trail that proves it is most of the work.

**On-premises** means hardware the customer controls, sometimes with no internet access. Everything above still applies, with tighter limits: the hardware is fixed rather than rented, updates become a release process, and monitoring cannot phone home. Model choice shifts towards what fits the machines that exist. That is where CPU numbers like ours become directly useful: a single-voice model at an RTF of 0.052 on four cores, from a 63.5 MB ONNX file, fits hardware a GPU-first model does not.

## Which licences gate a TTS deployment?

Every layer of the stack carries its own licence, and a deployment clears the gate only when all of them allow it. For the systems in the build-along:

| Layer           | Component                                                                                                                     | Licence                    |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Training data   | LJSpeech                                                                                                                      | Public domain              |
| Aligner weights | [torchaudio MMS_FA](https://docs.pytorch.org/audio/stable/generated/torchaudio.pipelines.MMS_FA.html) (data preparation only) | CC-BY-NC 4.0, per its docs |
| Vocoder weights | [speechbrain/tts-hifigan-ljspeech](https://huggingface.co/speechbrain/tts-hifigan-ljspeech)                                   | Apache-2.0                 |
| Voice weights   | [rhasspy/piper-voices](https://huggingface.co/rhasspy/piper-voices)                                                           | MIT                        |
| Runtime         | [piper-tts](https://github.com/OHF-voice/piper1-gpl)                                                                          | GPL-3.0-or-later           |

The aligner row is easy to miss: it never runs at serving time, but it shaped the training data, so read its terms before a commercial model depends on it. The Piper row is the one to remember: MIT weights and a GPL runtime make up one "model". Calling it behind your own API is one situation; under common readings of the GPL, shipping it inside a product installed at a client site can count as distribution, and then the runtime's terms travel with it. Check with counsel. [Dataset licensing](/learn/100x/tts/tts-dataset-licensing) covers the data side, including consent for any voice you clone or fine-tune.

## Where this leaves you

You have now built each stage of a TTS system: audio as numbers, a filtered dataset, a text front end, an acoustic model trained from random weights, a pretrained vocoder, and an [eval harness](/learn/100x/tts/tts-eval-harness) that scores it. The last unit added the parts you would need to take a model further: codec tokens, adaptation, cloning, serving and cost.

The tiny model remains a teaching model: 4.5 minutes of training on 0.74 h of audio, with a mean output WER of 21% against Piper's 1.9% on the same probes. The stages are the same ones a production system uses, and the measurements show how far that one small run got.

When the requirement is a specific language, dialect, domain or deployment boundary, our [custom speech models](/services/custom-speech-models) engagement follows the same order as this unit: acceptance metrics agreed in writing, then a data assessment, then training and evaluation against those metrics, then deployment.
