Pick the model everyone calls the smartest, and you will probably pick the wrong one for a phone call. Voice flips almost every rule that makes a model good in a chat window.

The language model is the brain of the agent: the software that takes the words the caller said and works out what to say back. On a chat screen, a brain that thinks for four seconds and then answers brilliantly is a great brain. On a call, four seconds of silence means the caller says "hello?" and starts over.

## The only speed that really matters is how fast it starts talking

When you type, you wait for the whole answer to appear. On a call it works differently: the agent starts speaking the first sentence out loud while the brain is still writing the third one. So the number that matters most is not how long the full answer takes to finish. It is how long before the very first word comes out.

That has a surprising result. A slower model that starts talking quickly beats a faster one that thinks in silence and then delivers the whole answer at once. The first one is already talking while the second is still deciding. (It does still have to keep up once it starts, or it will stall in the middle of a sentence, but the start is what the caller feels first.)

> On a call, a model that starts talking in a fifth of a second beats a smarter one that sits in silence for four seconds and then answers perfectly.

## It has to be brief, and that is about speed, not style

A voice answer should be one or two sentences. This is not a matter of taste. Every extra sentence is extra seconds the caller has to sit through and cannot easily interrupt. And because they are listening, not reading, they cannot skim back over a long answer, so a rambling reply is simply hard to follow. A model that writes three tidy paragraphs when you wanted one line is unusable on the phone, however good the paragraphs are. The best ones can be kept to a sentence or two. Some simply cannot stop, and that is a real mark against them.

## It should talk like a person, not read out a document

Chat models love to format things: bullet points, bold text, little headings, the odd emoji. On a screen that looks tidy. Read aloud it is a mess. The voice literally says "asterisk asterisk," or reads a bulleted list as one long breathless run-on. A model meant for voice writes plain spoken sentences. Check for this, because models built mainly for chat let that formatting slip into what they say constantly, and one stray list turns a competent agent into an obvious robot mid-call.

## The "thinking" setting is usually a trap on the phone

Many models now have a "thinking" or "reasoning" mode that pauses to work things out before answering. It makes them smarter on hard problems. On a call it is usually a trap, because all that thinking happens in silence, before a single word comes out. The caller hears nothing for several seconds, assumes the line dropped, and either repeats themselves or hangs up. Unless the agent can say something like "let me check that for you" while it thinks, the thinking time is just silence the caller has to sit through.

## It only knows what the ear passed along, and the ear makes mistakes

In the usual setup, the brain never actually hears the caller. It only sees the text that the listening part (speech to text, the part that writes down what the caller said) passed along, and on a phone line that text has mistakes in it: a wrong digit, a name turned into a similar-sounding word. A good model notices when something does not add up and asks a quick "sorry, was that five or nine?" A model that confidently acts on a misheard account number is worse than one that pauses to check, because it fails without ever realising it failed. (Some newer all-in-one models do hear the audio directly. That is a different way of building the agent, covered in [speech to speech](/learn/components/speech-to-speech).)

## It gets slower as the call goes on

There is a quiet problem that only shows up on long calls. Before every reply, the brain re-reads the whole conversation so far, and that conversation keeps getting longer. So the agent that answered in a snap at the start slows down turn by turn, and by the fortieth back-and-forth it is dragging, right when a tired caller is least patient. The fix is for the system to remember the earlier part of the call instead of re-reading it from scratch every time. That helps a lot, though it never quite makes a long call as fast as a short one. Test late in a long call, not just at the start, or you will never see it.

## The bottom line

One last thing worth knowing: the model is often not even the slow part. The wait while the system decides you have finished talking, which is [turn detection](/learn/components/turn-detection), can eat more time than the brain does. So the model to choose is the one that starts talking quickly, stays brief, sounds like a person, and still holds up at the fortieth back-and-forth, not the one that tops a test. The [latency calculator](/tools/latency-calculator), a small tool on this site, shows where each part's share of the delay sits, and a [production readiness review](/services/production-readiness-review) measures it on your own setup.
