Everything a voice agent does after this runs on the words it writes down: understanding the question, looking something up, replying. Get those words wrong and nothing later can fix it, so this quiet first step decides more calls than anything else in the stack.

Two quick terms before we go on. A voice agent is the automated voice that answers the phone and talks to callers. The model is just the industry's word for the software doing the listening.

And most of that software was never built for phone calls. It was built to type up a recording after the fact, like a court transcript. That is a different job, and that difference is the whole reason a model can look great in a demo and still fall apart on a live call.

## Typing up a recording is not the same as listening in a conversation

When you type up a recording, you have all the time in the world. You can wait for the whole sentence, think about it, and only then write it down. Accuracy is the only thing that matters, because a person reads it later.

A live phone call is the opposite. The agent has to start working out what the caller said while they are still talking, because the caller is waiting for an answer. Now two things matter at once: getting the words right, and getting them fast. And here is the problem: the faster the model goes, the more mistakes it makes. Nearly every model has to trade accuracy for speed, or speed for accuracy. A demo hides that trade. A real call puts it on display.

## One accuracy number can hide the callers it fails

Companies love to show a single score. "95% accurate." It sounds great. But that number is an average across thousands of test calls, and no real caller is average. Each caller only ever experiences their own call.

Think of a restaurant with 4.5 stars. That average can hide the fact that most nights are wonderful and a few nights are a disaster. If you walk in on a disaster night, the average does not help you.

Speech to text is the same. Two models can both score "95% accurate." One is good for everyone. The other is great for most people and terrible for anyone with an accent, an older voice, or a noisy room. On paper they look identical. In real life the second one quietly ruins one call in twenty, and nobody notices, because the average stays high.

> So do not ask how accurate it is on average. Ask how bad it gets on your worst callers. The worst case is what loses customers. The average just keeps the numbers looking calm.

## Why there is a pause before the agent answers

You have probably heard it: you finish talking, and there is a short, awkward silence before the agent replies. Part of that silence comes from this step, so it helps to know which part.

The model does two things, at two different speeds. While you are still talking, it shows its best guess of your words almost straight away. That part is fast. Then, once you stop, it takes a moment to finalise: to lock in the finished, tidied-up version of what you said. That finalising moment should be quick, because nothing else can use your words until they are final.

Here is the honest limit of what this part controls. Speech to text does not decide whether you have actually finished talking or only paused to think. That is a separate job, done by a different part of the system, and it is where most of the awkward silence actually comes from. It has its own page: [turn detection](/learn/components/turn-detection). For speech to text, what you want is simple. Once you have stopped, it should hand over the final words quickly, so everything after it can get moving.

There is one more speech-to-text quirk worth knowing. While you talk, it keeps updating its live guess, and sometimes it changes a word it already showed, like autocomplete correcting itself. If the agent tries to get a head start by acting on an early guess, and that guess then changes, it has acted on the wrong words. A model that keeps its guesses steady is more useful than a slightly faster one that keeps changing its mind.

## Phone calls sound worse than the demo, and that matters

Here is something almost nobody tests for. A phone call throws away a big part of the sound. Phones were built to carry just enough to understand a voice, so they cut out the high notes. That is why "s," "f," and "th" all sound alike on a phone, and why you sometimes have to spell things out.

The model has the exact same trouble. But nearly every accuracy test is done on clean recordings made in a quiet studio, not on scratchy phone audio. So that "95% accurate" number was measured on audio your caller will never actually use. On a real phone line the same model does clearly worse, and the demo never showed it.

The fix is easy to say: test it on real phone-call audio, not clean recordings. If the company selling it only shows you clean-audio numbers, treat them as the best case, not the real one.

## Noise, and the worst kind of noise

Real callers are in cars, kitchens, and busy offices. Background noise makes everything harder, but not all noise is equal. The worst kind is other people talking. A TV or a crowd behind the caller confuses the model far more than steady noise like a fan or engine noise from driving, because chatter sounds like speech too, and the model cannot tell which voice to follow.

So when you test, do not just try a quiet room. Turn up real background chatter and watch how quickly the model falls apart. There is a point where it stops being usable. You want to find that point before a caller does.

## The words that matter are the ones it gets wrong

This is the failure that catches almost everyone, and it is sneaky.

The model will happily get all the small talk right, then mishear the one thing that actually mattered: the account number, the product name, the medication, the person's name. It hears "one five zero" as "one hundred and fifty," or turns a real surname into a common word that sounds similar. The overall accuracy score barely moves, because it is one word out of many. But that one word was the whole point of the call. Now the agent looks up the wrong thing, books the wrong thing, or charges the wrong amount.

The worst part is that it does not know it got it wrong. It sounds just as sure about the mistake as about everything else. An agent that gets the chit-chat perfect and quietly corrupts every account number is worse than no agent at all.

Because no system is perfect on these, the real safety net is to read the important bits back: "That is account four one five, is that right?" A good agent confirms the things that matter instead of assuming it heard them correctly.

## The fix is usually a cheat sheet, not a bigger model

When this happens, the instinct is to go and buy a smarter, more expensive model. It usually does not help, because those rare words, your product names and your customers' names, are rare for the big model too. It has barely heard them either.

What actually works is giving the model a cheat sheet: a short list of the words and names it should expect on this call, handed to it just before it listens. Told what to look for, it gets those exact words right far more often, and it costs almost nothing. That is why the smart question for your business is not "which model is the most accurate," but "can I give it a list of my words."

And when even a good model with a good cheat sheet still cannot keep up, that is the sign you need a model built specially for your callers, using recordings of real calls like yours, rather than another off-the-shelf one.
